Project Overview

The Challenge

Our accounting SaaS platform's existing survey system was creating friction for users and operational burden for our engineering team. Users encountered poorly-timed questions about exams they hadn't yet taken, unclear optional vs. required fields, and a confusing experience that didn't account for the significant differences between certification types (CPA, CMA, etc.). Meanwhile, engineers were hand-coding each survey from scratch, making iteration slow and resource-intensive.

My Role

UX Designer responsible for end-to-end survey experience redesign, including content strategy, survey logic, delivery mechanisms, and cross-functional coordination with Product and Engineering.

The Goal

  1. User-facing: Create a timely, contextually relevant survey experience that adapts to each certification path
  2. Business-facing: Increase score report acquisition and user response rates while establishing a scalable survey infrastructure
  3. Operational: Enable non-engineers to build and iterate on surveys independently using a third-party platform

Navigating Constraints Through Collaboration

This project required careful balance between competing stakeholder needs. When friction emerged between the PM's requirement for an open-source platform and Engineering's preference for paid solutions with more robust features, I took on a facilitator role.

My approach: I assembled a comparative analysis deck for the engineering team to present to our Director of Operations. While open-source options met our data privacy requirements (we needed a platform that wouldn't store user data), they would significantly increase engineering implementation time—we'd still need to build custom infrastructure for data handling and routing. The analysis also documented which platforms included built-in dashboard capabilities versus requiring custom builds.

Given that the project ultimately took a year with several interruptions *using* a third-party platform, an open-source solution would have extended the timeline considerably. This deck became the foundation for getting organizational buy-in on Formsort and demonstrated how design can surface the true cost-benefit tradeoffs across technical and business considerations.

Designing for Context: The Three-Path Solution

Rather than creating a one-size-fits-all survey, I identified three distinct user states that required different experiences:

Completed Exam

Focus on score collection and post-exam feedback.

Not Yet Taken

Gather preparation insights without premature questions.

On a Study Break

Understand barriers sensitively (including "personal reasons" as an option to balance business needs with user privacy)

The design process:

  • Cataloged questions we needed answered based on internal knowledge gaps and previous user feedback
  • Created high-fidelity wireframes showing all three conditional flows
  • Developed a logic diagram for engineers and QA testers to validate branching without manually testing every path
  • Built a timing chart showing when each certification type should receive which survey variant

This systematic approach ensured everyone understood not just what we were building, but why certain questions appeared for certain users at certain times.

Strategic Pivot: Rethinking Delivery

Initially, we explored modal delivery at three key points in the user journey. Through collaborative discussion with Product and Engineering, we uncovered a critical insight: our platform already used modals frequently for other interventions, and adding another would create notification fatigue.

The solution: We repurposed an existing header element that appeared at course completion—a location I had previously redesigned with another designer three years prior. This turned out to be a fortunate decision: the previous survey in that location had strong response rates, but no one had connected that success to the placement itself.

For CPA and CMA users with dramatically different exam timelines, we supplemented with targeted email campaigns. I researched survey notification patterns on Mobbin and used AI to help draft contextually appropriate copy for each user segment.

Building Bridges with Visual Communication

One of my most valuable contributions was creating artifacts that made complex logic accessible to non-designers:

  • Conditional logic flowcharts allowed testers and engineers to evaluate survey behavior without constant manual testing
  • Timing charts aligned stakeholders on when different certification paths would trigger surveys
  • Delivery mechanism presentations explored email vs. modal options with visual mockups

These tools transformed abstract concepts into concrete discussion points, enabling faster feedback cycles and making cross-functional team members feel invested in the outcome.

Validation & Iteration

I facilitated review sessions with testers, PMs, and engineers at key milestones. One critical piece of feedback reshaped the score request question: not all certifications provide numerical scores upon passing. Users were confused by requests for information they didn't have, even when marked optional.

The fix: I added conditional logic to only surface the score question for certification types that actually issue scores, reducing confusion and improving data quality.

What I Learned

Small changes compound. We didn't initially plan to update the survey's header location—we only intended to refresh its content. But recognizing the placement's previous success and preserving it proved as valuable as the new survey itself.

Visual artifacts are collaboration tools. Flowcharts and diagrams didn't just document decisions—they invited participation. Testers could spot logic errors, engineers could flag technical concerns earlier, and PMs could validate business requirements before I invested time in detailed design.

Balanced advocacy matters. Users needed privacy and clarity. The business needed data. Engineering needed feasibility. My role wasn't to champion one stakeholder over others, but to find the design solution that served all three—like offering "personal reasons" as a response option that gave counselors a touchpoint without forcing disclosure.

Moving Forward

This project reinforced the power of communication artifacts—flowcharts, timing diagrams, and logic maps— when all team members are aligned and engaged. The success I saw here has influenced how I scope similar projects: I now draft design briefs upfront to establish shared understanding of deliverables and success criteria from day one. And I've learned to question assumptions about "small" changes—sometimes repositioning existing UI elements creates more value than designing new ones.
